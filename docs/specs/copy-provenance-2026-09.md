# Copy provenance – what the corpus may learn from, and what it may never take

Ruled 10.09 («делайте»), written so the law is recorded rather than implied. The 10.09 editorial
review's copyright assessment found no copied dramatic dialogue in the shipped corpus and asked
for exactly this note.

## The law

1. **Films, matches and transcriptions are STRUCTURE STUDY, never a phrase bank.** What may be
   learned from them: scene function, escalation, interruption, subtext, conversational
   asymmetry, burnout structure, what a character refuses to say – and TONE (the booth that says
   «I hope that is a victory cigar» is a register reference, not a line to lift).
2. **No distinctive expression, no dialogue sequences, no recognisable character imitation**
   crosses into the corpus. Short commonplace phrases and general styles are outside copyright's
   reach; distinctive expression is not – the working distinction is the U.S. Copyright Office's
   Circular 33, and when in doubt the line is not taken.
3. The standing house rules stand beside this: fictional tournament and organisation names;
   no constructible real-player surnames; every player-facing word the owner's (invariant 4).
4. The research corpus (`docs/research/`) stays exactly where it is – as evidence and
   calibration. ⚠ The owner's film transcriptions with dialogue are NOT in the repo as of 10.09
   (searched: research, raw, lore, the film worktrees) – he re-shares them and they land here as
   `reference` before the broadcaster wave builds. A line's road from research into the game runs
   through a writer's own sentence and the owner's вычитка, never through paste.

## Where the tone references live

The childhood prologue is the corpus's own tonal benchmark (the 10.09 review's finding, adopted).
External references – commentary booths, tennis film transcriptions – inform REGISTER per the
law above; the broadcaster's re-cut (the-way-she-sounds C4) names them explicitly as tone, not
text.
